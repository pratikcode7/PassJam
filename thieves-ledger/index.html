      The Truth About Passwords

      Here the ledger drops its act. What follows is the real advice from NIST Special Publication 800-63B, the United States guidance on passwords and sign-in, put in plain words.

      Length matters more than strange symbols

      A long passphrase of ordinary words is stronger than a short, messy password. “four rusty camels drink at noon” is easier to remember and much harder to guess than “Tr0ub4dor!”.

      Changing passwords every 90 days does not make them safer

      Forced changes teach people to add a number to the end of the old one. A password should be changed when there is a sign it was stolen, not because a calendar says so.

      Composition rules make passwords harder to remember, not harder to crack

      “One uppercase, one symbol” turns “password” into “Password1!”, which is one of the first things an attacker tries. The rules only make you reach for a sticky note.

      Sites should check new passwords against leaked lists

      Billions of passwords have leaked from old break-ins. A good site refuses any new password that appears on those lists, along with dictionary words and easy patterns like “aaaaaa” or “123456”.

      Sites should allow paste

      Password managers fill in long, random passwords by pasting them. Blocking paste pushes people back to short passwords they can type from memory. The Captain's paste ban was the villain all along.

      Use a password manager and turn on two-factor sign-in

      A password manager remembers a long, different password for every site, so one leak cannot open the rest. A second step, such as a code from an app or a security key, stops a thief who has learned your password.
